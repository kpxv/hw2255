\section*{Abstract}

The purpose of this exercise was to examine an approximately linear circuit
with unknown internals and determine the Thevenin equivalent resistance and
voltage. The circuit was simulated using LTSpice. Several DC Bias point
simulations were conducted. The simulated values were used to estimate the
Thevenin equivalent voltage and resistance. The circuit was implemented in
hardware by connecting a resistive load to a function generator. The voltage
across the load and the current through the load were measured to calculate
and approximate Thevenin equivalent voltage and resistance. The estimated and
measured Thevenin equivalent voltages and resistances were similar; the
exercise was successful.

\section{Introduction and Theory}

The objective of this laboratory was to apply several loads to an unknown
circuit; to measure the voltage across and current through each load; and to
find an approximate Thevenin equivalent resistance and voltage to represent
the circuit.

\subsection{Theory: Circuit Investigated}

There exist several methods to determine the Thevenin equivalent voltage and
resistance of a known circuit; however, none of them are practical to
experimentally determine the Thevenin equivalents of a circuit with unknown
elements. Neither test signals nor inspection may be used, because the circuit
is unknown. Shorting the circuit may damage it.

Instead, multiple resistive loads may be applied. By measuring the voltage
across and the current through each load, the Thevenin resistance can be
approximated by plotting the measured voltages with respect to the measured
currents and examining the slope of the line of best fit. The Thevenin voltage
can be found at the $y$-intercept of the line of best fit.

\section{Conclusion}

The Thevenin equivalences of an unknown circuit were simulated and measured
by connecting several load resistors of varying resistances and finding the
ratio of change in voltage over change in current. Although other methods for
determining Thevenin equivalences may fail during experimental use, load
resistances are a safe way to approximate them. From the experiment, it was
determined that having a positive (non-zero) Thevenin resistance decreases the
measured load voltage from the voltage source; and that the higher the
Thevenin resistance, the greater the decrease in measured load voltage. All
measurements were within reasonable ranges of their simulated counterparts. The
exercise was successful.
